The premise

Searching without a map

Metaheuristics find good-enough solutions to hard problems where brute force is impossible and gradients don't exist. They split into two camps — but share one core tension:

  • Exploration vs. exploitation — venture into the unknown vs. refine what you've found
  • Single-solution (S-metaheuristics) — improve one candidate along a trajectory: Random Search, Hill Climbing, SA, Tabu, GRASP, VNS
  • Population-based (P-metaheuristics) — evolve many candidates at once: GA, ES, DE, EDA, PSO, ACO, ABC
  • Derivative-free & stochastic — they only evaluate fitness, and use randomness to escape local traps

P1 · Genetic Algorithm · evolutionary

Survival of the fittest

How it works

  • Selection — fitter parents are chosen to breed
  • Crossover — parents swap genetic material
  • Mutation — random tweaks keep variety alive
  • Elitism — the best survive untouched

Aggressive exploiter — converges fast, but elitist pressure can collapse diversity onto one peak.

Best for
  • High-dimensional continuous spaces
  • Fast convergence & combinatorial (TSP)
Avoid when
  • Landscapes have many peaks
  • You need a diverse solution set

P2 · Evolution Strategies · evolutionary

Evolution that tunes itself

How it works

  • Real vectors — operates directly on continuous parameters
  • Gaussian mutation — perturb by a normal step
  • Self-adaptation — the step size σ evolves alongside the solution
  • (μ,λ) / (μ+λ) — select μ parents from λ offspring; CMA-ES adapts the full covariance
x′ = x + σ·𝒩(0, C)  ·  σ, C self-adapt

The continuous-optimization specialist. CMA-ES is one of the strongest black-box optimizers — it learns the shape of the landscape and rescales its search to match.

Best for
  • Black-box / ill-conditioned continuous problems
  • When you want self-tuning, few manual params
Avoid when
  • Discrete / combinatorial structure
  • Very high-D (covariance cost grows)

P3 · Differential Evolution · evolutionary

Difference is the engine

How it works

  • Mutation — scale the difference of random vectors
  • Crossover — mix donor with target
  • Selection — keep whichever is fitter
v = x_r1 + F · (x_r2 − x_r3)

The benchmark heavyweight. Self-adapting variants like L-SHADE have won CEC competitions and scale reliably to thousands of dimensions.

Best for
  • Large-scale continuous (100+ D)
  • When best quality is the goal
Avoid when
  • Discrete / combinatorial problems
  • Fitness is noisy

P4 · Estimation of Distribution · evolutionary · model-based

Evolution by probability

How it works

  • No crossover/mutation — replaced by a statistical model
  • Estimate — build a probability model of the best solutions
  • Sample — draw the next population from the model
  • Update — re-fit the model each generation

UMDA · PBIL · BOA (Bayesian network)

Learns the structure of good solutions — capturing dependencies between variables that blind operators miss. The model itself is an explainable by-product.

Best for
  • Problems with variable interactions / structure
  • When you want an interpretable model
Avoid when
  • Compute is tight (model-building cost)
  • Dependencies exceed the model's order

P5 · Particle Swarm · swarm

Follow the flock

How it works

  • Inertia — particles keep their momentum
  • Cognitive — pull toward personal best
  • Social — pull toward swarm best
v ← w·v + c₁(pbest−x) + c₂(gbest−x)

Lightweight and elegant — few parameters, naturally parallel. Can converge prematurely when the swarm rushes the same peak.

Best for
  • Smooth continuous optimization
  • Minimal tuning, parallel / real-time
Avoid when
  • Highly constrained combinatorial
  • Premature convergence is a risk

P6 · Ant Colony · swarm

Trails do the talking

How it works

  • Construct — ants walk a graph, building solutions edge by edge
  • Pheromone — each path is marked in proportion to its quality
  • Evaporation — old trails decay so bad routes fade
  • Feedback — good edges attract more ants
pij ∝ [τij]α · [ηij]β

The graph specialist. Born for the TSP (Dorigo, 1992), it shines on routing and discrete problems where a solution is a path. ACS and Max–Min Ant System sharpen the balance.

Best for
  • Routing & combinatorial (TSP, VRP, scheduling)
  • Dynamic problems — trails adapt online
Avoid when
  • Smooth continuous / very high-D
  • You can't afford to tune α, β, ρ, Q

P7 · Bee Colony · swarm

The wisdom of the hive

How it works

  • Employed bees — exploit known food sources
  • Onlookers — probabilistically recruit to rich sources (waggle dance)
  • Scouts — abandon exhausted sources, explore anew

Patient explorer. Keeps its population diverse far longer, which makes it strong at escaping local optima and tackling deceptive, multi-peak landscapes.

Best for
  • Multimodal optimization, multiple good solutions
  • Constrained combinatorial (VRP, TSP)
Avoid when
  • You need very fast convergence
  • Compute budget is tight

S1 · Random Search · baseline

The control everyone must beat

How it works

  • Sample — draw candidates uniformly (or random-walk)
  • Keep best — remember the best seen so far
  • No memory of structure — never exploits what it learned

Embarrassingly simple and parallel — and a surprisingly stubborn control in high dimensions. If a method can't beat random search, it isn't earning its complexity.

Best for
  • Baselines & sanity checks, tiny budgets
  • Embarrassingly parallel hyper-parameter search
Avoid when
  • Any structure can be exploited (almost always)

S2 · Hill Climbing · local search

Always go uphill

How it works

  • Neighborhood — examine nearby solutions
  • Move — step to the best improving neighbor
  • Stop — halt at a local optimum
  • Variants — steepest-ascent, first-improvement, random-restart

The pure exploiter and the core of every local search. Fast and memory-light — but trapped at the first local optimum unless you add restarts or an escape rule.

Best for
  • Convex / unimodal problems, quick polish
  • The local step inside a memetic algorithm
Avoid when
  • Rugged, multimodal or plateaued landscapes

S3 · Simulated Annealing · local search

Cool down to settle

How it works

  • Accept worse moves — with probability P = e−Δ/T
  • Cool — lower the temperature T ← α·T over time
  • Explore → exploit — hot = wander, cold = refine
P(accept) = exp(−Δ ⁄ T)  ·  T ← α·T

Escapes local optima through probabilistic uphill moves, with an asymptotic global-optimum guarantee. The cooling schedule is everything.

Best for
  • Rugged single-solution & combinatorial (TSP, layout)
  • When a good cooling schedule is available
Avoid when
  • Tight time budgets, need population diversity

S4 · Tabu Search · local search

Memory that forbids backtracking

How it works

  • Best move — always move to the best neighbor, even if worse
  • Tabu list — recently-visited moves are forbidden (short-term memory)
  • Aspiration — override the tabu if a move is good enough
  • Long-term memory — intensify & diversify the search

Memory-driven and largely deterministic — it escapes local optima by refusing to revisit, instead of relying on randomness. A workhorse on structured combinatorial problems.

Best for
  • Scheduling, routing, structured combinatorial
Avoid when
  • Continuous black-box, hard-to-tune tenure

S5 · GRASP · multi-start local search

Greedy, randomized, repeated

How it works

  • Construct — greedy build from a restricted candidate list (RCL)
  • Randomize — pick from the RCL at random for variety
  • Local search — polish each constructed solution
  • Repeat — many independent restarts, keep the best

Greedy Randomized Adaptive Search Procedure — diversification comes from randomized seeds, intensification from local search. Each start is independent, so it parallelizes trivially.

Best for
  • Combinatorial (covering, scheduling, routing)
  • Parallel restarts, good quality fast
Avoid when
  • Construction is hard to randomize; continuous spaces

S6 · VNS / LNS · local search

Change the neighborhood

How it works

  • Shake — jump randomly into the k-th neighborhood
  • Local search — descend to that neighborhood's optimum
  • Move or grow — better? reset k=1; else k++ (widen)
  • LNS — ruin-and-recreate large chunks of the solution

Escapes local optima by systematically switching neighborhood structures rather than tuning randomness. General VNS and Large Neighborhood Search power many modern routing solvers.

Best for
  • Combinatorial / routing (VRP), large-scale via LNS
  • When several neighborhood moves exist
Avoid when
  • Only one natural neighborhood; smooth continuous

The mindset

Characterize the problem first

No algorithm is "best" in a vacuum — the structure of your problem picks it for you. Answer these six questions before writing a line of code.

1 · Search space

Continuous? → ES · DE · PSO
Combinatorial / graph? → ACO · Tabu · VNS
Permutation (TSP)? → ACO · GA · VNS

2 · Landscape

One basin (unimodal)? → Hill Climbing · DE
Many peaks? → Bee Colony · SA · GA
Deceptive? → SA · ABC · restarts

3 · Dimensionality

< 50 D? → GA · PSO · ABC
50–500 D? → DE · ES
> 500 D? → DE (L-SHADE)

4 · Budget

Few evaluations? → DE · CMA-ES
Expensive fitness? → EDA · surrogates
Plenty / parallel? → ABC · PSO · GRASP

5 · Constraints

Hard constraints? → Tabu · penalty / repair
Feasibility hard? → GRASP · ACO construct
Dynamic / online? → ACO · VNS

6 · Output needed

One best answer? → DE · GA · CMA-ES
Diverse set? → Bee Colony · niching GA
Want a model? → EDA

The mindset

A five-step triage

Run this loop top to bottom — each step narrows the field, and step 5 sends you back to step 1 to verify.

  • 1 · Establish a baseline — run Random Search and plain Hill Climbing. Whatever you pick next must beat them, or the complexity isn't earning its keep.
  • 2 · Classify the space — continuous → evolutionary / swarm (DE, ES, PSO); combinatorial → trajectory / construction (Tabu, VNS, ACO, GRASP).
  • 3 · Probe the landscape — multimodal or deceptive? add diversity (Bee Colony) or escape moves (SA, VNS). Smooth & unimodal? exploit hard (Hill Climbing, DE).
  • 4 · Match the budget — few evals → DE / CMA-ES; expensive fitness → EDA / surrogates; massively parallel → GRASP / PSO / ABC.
  • 5 · Hybridize, tune, re-measure — bolt a local search onto a global method (memetic), tune only the parameters that matter, then re-check against the step-1 baseline.

Beyond the rivalry

Hybrids win in practice

The strongest modern results combine paradigms rather than picking one:

  • Memetic (GA / ES + local search) — global reach refined by SA, Tabu or VNS polish
  • PSO + DE — particle motion with differential mutation (CEC winners)
  • GRASP + path-relinking — restarts that learn from elite solutions
  • ACO / EDA + local search — model-built solutions sharpened by neighborhood moves

Case study

Seven of these, one real problem

Everything above is the map. This is the territory: the Set Orienteering Problem — pick a subset of clustered customers and a tour through them that maximises collected profit inside a fixed travel budget.

max  Σ pi·yi    s.t.   Σ tij ≤ Tmax

A cluster pays its profit once, collected by visiting any one member. So the problem is a knapsack (which clusters?) welded to a generalised TSP (in what order?) — and each decision reshapes the other. NP-hard; exact methods stop around a hundred customers.

  • 7 metaheuristics — SA, Tabu, GRASP, GA, Memetic, ACO, PSO
  • One shared representation, objective and feasibility module
  • Pure Rust, zero crates, strictly single-threaded
  • 43 instances × 10 runs × 30 s, one protocol for all
  • Every route re-verified by a checker sharing no code with the solvers

Reading a failure properly

GRASP wasn't slow — it was leaving money on the table

GRASP filled only 77.4 % of the budget while everything else sat above 96 %. Since the objective is profit under a budget, unspent budget is unclaimed profit. Diagnosis by controlled experiment, not by guessing.

The suspicion

Its local search is DP vertex selection + 2-opt. Both only reduce cost. Nothing ever re-spends the budget they free.

The test

Take its own emitted routes, apply one greedy refill — cheapest-position insertion by profit-per-detour. No re-routing, no re-selection.

The result

Improves 43 / 43 instances. Gap 31.77 % → 12.85 %, budget 77.4 % → 98.8 %. Sixty per cent of its gap was never algorithmic.

The control

Tabu, GA and PSO give back exactly zero — their tours are already saturated. That contrast is what turns an observation into a diagnosis.

Throughput was ruled out first: GRASP completes 2.5–3.7 million iterations in thirty seconds on the 200-node instances. Not compute — reachability. With those operators a saturated solution cannot be reached at all.

The lesson

Where the theory held, and where it didn't

Held up

  • No free lunch, confirmed. The scorecard earlier rates GA and PSO alike; on this problem they split by a factor of ten on mean gap.
  • Hybridise. The winner is PSO only in its outer loop — a random-key decoder wrapping a local-search stack. Bare PSO loses 37.8 %.
  • Constraint handling matters more than the metaphor. Methods that never generate an infeasible solution spent their whole budget searching instead of repairing.

Didn't

  • "ACO excels on combinatorial problems" — here it finished fifth at 5.1 %. True of the paradigm, not automatically of your implementation.
  • "GRASP: good quality, moderate effort" — 31.8 %, from one missing operator. A benchmark measures an implementation, never a method.
  • Fast convergence ≠ good answers. GA and SA stop in under a second and call it done; PSO uses all thirty and wins.

No free lunch

No single algorithm wins everywhere — the landscape decides the champion. Match the method to the problem's structure, lean on single-solution search when memory is tight, populations when you can afford diversity, and reach for hybrids when one paradigm isn't enough.
