Part II — Snap Taxi Sharing Optimization

30 points · 3 sub-questions × 10 points each

Context: To reduce urban traffic congestion, the Snap taxi company has designed a shared taxi system. Taxis accept passengers willing to pay less in exchange for sharing rides. The company wants to maximize revenue while minimizing the number of taxis in use.

City model: All city points are known in advance along with each passenger's travel time. Each point is accessible 24/7 and a taxi can reach any point from any other.

Trigger mechanism: Every minute, all passenger requests are collected. One taxi is assigned to handle them. Goal: serve all requests with one taxi while minimizing total travel distance and maximizing total revenue per route.

Assignment rule: If 2 or 5 requests arrive, one taxi is assigned. Two objectives are evaluated per taxi per route: total distance per person and total money collected per route.

Given Route Structures (from exam)

stop +2 ————————— stop -2 ————— stop ...   → Taxi (1) — group A only
stop +5 ————————— stop -5 ————— stop ...   → Taxi (2) — group B only
stop +2 —— stop +5 —— stop -2 —— stop -5 —— stop ... → Shared Taxi (Route 3)

+N = pickup for N passengers  |  -N = drop-off for N passengers  |  Route 3 interleaves both groups in one trip

Part (a) — 10 pts Using a suitable global metaheuristic, model this problem — explain the chromosome representation and fitness function ▼

Chosen Algorithm: Genetic Algorithm (GA)

GA is the best fit because this is fundamentally a combinatorial route sequencing problem — finding the optimal order to visit pickup and drop-off stops. GA's permutation chromosome naturally encodes this ordering, and its operators (OX crossover, swap mutation) are well-suited to explore valid route orderings without needing gradient information.

Problem Variables

  • Group A: 2 passengers — 1 pickup stop (+2) and 1 drop-off stop (-2)
  • Group B: 5 passengers — 1 pickup stop (+5) and 1 drop-off stop (-5)

Hard constraint: For every group i, pickup (+i) must be visited before drop-off (-i). Passengers cannot be dropped off before being picked up.

Chromosome Representation — Permutation Encoding

Each chromosome is an ordered sequence of stop IDs. Length = 2 × number of groups = 4 genes total.

// Valid chromosome examples: [ +2, +5, -2, -5 ] ← pickup both first, then drop both [ +2, -2, +5, -5 ] ← complete group A, then group B [ +5, +2, -5, -2 ] ← pickup B first, then A, drop B, then A // INVALID (violates precedence): [ -2, +2, +5, -5 ] ← group A dropped before picked up!

Validity rule: index(+i) < index(-i) must hold for all i.

Fitness Function — Bi-objective

// Minimize total route distance: f₁(x) = Σ dist(stop_k → stop_{k+1}) for all consecutive stops // Maximize total revenue per route: f₂(x) = Σ fare_i for all passengers served // Option A — weighted sum: Fitness = w₁ × (1/f₁) + w₂ × f₂ // Option B — Pareto-based (NSGA-II): Rank by Pareto dominance + crowding distance → full trade-off front

Genetic Operators

Part (b) — 10 pts Suggest the method proposed in this paper — what does it propose and why is it better? ▼

The Proposed Method: Collaborative Shared Routing (Route 3)

The paper proposes replacing the two-taxi independent approach with a single shared taxi that intelligently interleaves pickups and drop-offs from both request groups in one optimized trip.

Instead of two independent routes (Taxi 1 for group A, Taxi 2 for group B), one taxi follows: +2 → +5 → -2 → -5 or another optimal interleaving — serving all 7 passengers in one trip.

Route Comparison

Routes 1+2 (independent)2 taxisLower — fewer passengers per taxiTwo taxis on road
Route 3 (shared)1 taxiHigher — all 7 passengers in one routeOne taxi reduces traffic
Key insight: By merging both groups, the company achieves both objectives simultaneously — fewer taxis (reduces congestion) and higher per-route revenue (fixed costs shared across more passengers). The two objectives reinforce each other rather than conflicting.

Why a Metaheuristic Is Needed

The metaheuristic finds the optimal interleaving sequence for the shared route. The naive sequence may not be the shortest — depending on the geographic positions of stops, other orderings may produce significantly less total distance traveled. Since the number of valid orderings grows factorially with passengers, exact methods are intractable at scale.

2. Objective Comparison

Objective 1 — Distance: Independent routes add no intergroup detour. Shared route may add detour overhead, but eliminates redundant parallel travel when routes overlap geographically.
Objective 2 — Revenue: Independent taxis earn from fewer passengers per trip (lower revenue density). Shared taxi collects from all 7 passengers → higher revenue density → fixed costs shared across more people → lower per-person price → competitive advantage → more demand.

3. Representation and Operators

  • Independent: Two separate GAs, smaller chromosomes, simpler constraints, faster convergence — but solves the wrong problem (ignores cross-group sharing).
  • Shared: Single GA, combined chromosome, requires OX/PMX crossover and repair mechanism — more complex, but finds genuinely better solutions.

4. Convergence Cost

Independent approach converges faster (smaller space). Shared approach needs a larger population (200–500) and more generations (500–1000). However, this extra cost is paid once per minute per request batch — trivially fast on modern hardware.

5. Scalability

As simultaneous requests grow, the shared approach scales better: each additional passenger reduces per-person fixed cost and increases per-route revenue. The independent approach scales linearly in taxi count — exactly what the company wants to minimize.

Recommendation

Implement the shared approach (Route 3) with: permutation chromosome, OX crossover, swap mutation + repair, tournament selection (k=2), and NSGA-II if a full Pareto front of distance/revenue trade-offs is desired.

Problem 1 — University Course Scheduling (30 pts)

A university must schedule n courses into t time slots across r rooms. Each course has an enrollment count and a preferred room type (lab, lecture hall, seminar). Constraints: (1) No student can attend two courses in the same time slot. (2) A room cannot host two courses simultaneously. (3) Each course must be assigned to a room with sufficient capacity. The university wants to minimize student conflicts (cases where a student needs to be in two places at once) and maximize room utilization (rooms used at full capacity as often as possible).

Approach A: Assign all courses randomly to slots and rooms, then fix conflicts one by one (greedy repair)
Approach B: Use a Genetic Algorithm with a permutation chromosome encoding slot+room assignment pairs
Approach C (proposed): Use a hybrid GA + Local Search (Memetic Algorithm) with constraint-aware crossover
Part (a) — 10 pts Model the course scheduling problem with a suitable metaheuristic — define the representation and fitness function ▼

Chosen Algorithm: Genetic Algorithm (GA)

Course scheduling is a classic constraint satisfaction + combinatorial optimization problem. The search space is the set of all possible assignments of courses to (time slot, room) pairs — exponentially large. GA is ideal because it can simultaneously explore many candidate schedules and optimize both objectives (conflict minimization, room utilization) without requiring a mathematical gradient of the scheduling objective.

Chromosome Representation

Each chromosome encodes a complete schedule. Use an integer array of length n (one gene per course):

// Each gene encodes a (time_slot, room) pair as a single integer: Gene_i = slot_id × R + room_id where R = number of rooms // Example: 5 courses, 3 slots, 4 rooms (gene value = slot*4 + room): Chromosome: [ 2, 7, 0, 5, 11 ] Decoded: [ (0,2), (1,3), (0,0), (1,1), (2,3) ] Meaning: Course 1→slot0/room2, Course 2→slot1/room3, ... // Alternative: two separate arrays (clearer but longer): Slots: [ 0, 1, 0, 1, 2 ] Rooms: [ 2, 3, 0, 1, 3 ]

Fitness Function — Bi-objective

// Objective 1 — minimize student conflicts: f₁(x) = Σ conflicts(s, t) for all students s, all time slots t where conflicts(s,t) = max(0, courses_with_student_s_in_slot_t - 1) // Objective 2 — maximize room utilization: f₂(x) = Σ (enrollment_c / capacity_room_c) for all courses c // Penalty for hard constraints (room capacity violated): penalty(x) = λ × Σ max(0, enrollment_c - capacity_assigned_room_c) // Combined fitness (weighted sum): Fitness(x) = -w₁ × f₁(x) + w₂ × f₂(x) - penalty(x) [maximize]

Genetic Operators

OperatorChoiceWhy
CrossoverUniform crossoverEach gene (course assignment) is independent of others — no ordering relationship. Uniform crossover correctly mixes independent assignments.
MutationRandom resettingRandomly reassign one course to a new (slot, room) pair. Simple and effective for integer encoding.
SelectionTournament (k=3)Controlled pressure without scaling. Works well across the wide range of fitness values that scheduling produces.
RepairGreedy repair post-crossoverAfter crossover, detect room double-bookings and reassign conflicting courses to available slots/rooms.
Part (b) — 10 pts What is the proposed Memetic Algorithm approach and why is it better than plain GA? ▼

The Proposed Method: Memetic Algorithm (MA) = GA + Local Search

The proposed approach (Approach C) is a Memetic Algorithm — a GA where each offspring, after being created by crossover and mutation, is immediately improved by a local search procedure before being added to the population.

Why Plain GA Struggles with Scheduling

Standard GA operates at the population level — it mixes and mutates genes but doesn't systematically improve individual solutions. For scheduling, many constraint violations can be fixed by simple local adjustments (e.g., swap one course to a different slot). GA leaves this on the table — it might "accidentally" fix it through crossover but has no mechanism to deliberately do so.

The Memetic addition: After crossover/mutation produces an offspring, apply a deterministic local search (e.g., 1-opt: try reassigning each course to every other slot/room pair and keep the best improvement) before evaluating fitness. This ensures every individual in the population is a local optimum — much higher baseline quality.

What Constraint-Aware Crossover Adds

Standard uniform crossover doesn't know about student enrollment data. Constraint-aware crossover checks, before performing a gene swap, whether the swap would create a room capacity violation or a guaranteed conflict — and chooses a different gene pair if so. This dramatically reduces the repair overhead and produces higher-quality offspring.

Comparison

AspectPlain GA (Approach B)Memetic Algorithm (Approach C)
Per-individual qualityRaw — may have many fixable violationsLocally optimal — violations minimized by LS
Crossover operatorBlind to constraintsConstraint-aware — avoids producing known-bad offspring
Convergence speedSlower — poor individuals drag evolutionFaster — higher average population quality
Computational cost per generationLowerHigher (LS adds cost per individual)
Part (c) — 10 pts Pre-implementation analysis: compare Approach B (plain GA) vs Approach C (Memetic) — which would you implement and why? ▼

1. Search Space

For n=50 courses, t=10 slots, r=20 rooms, the search space has (t×r)^n = 200^50 ≈ 10^115 possible schedules. Both approaches must navigate this — but the Memetic approach reduces the effective search space by only considering local optima, which form a much smaller and higher-quality subset.

2. Fitness Landscape

Scheduling fitness landscapes are notoriously rugged — many local optima close in quality to the global optimum, separated by barriers requiring multiple simultaneous changes. Plain GA's global search helps here; pure local search would get trapped. The Memetic combination is ideal: GA provides landscape-level exploration, LS provides within-basin exploitation.

3. Constraint Density

Scheduling is a highly constrained problem. A large fraction of the search space is infeasible. This makes blind crossover in plain GA very wasteful — it frequently produces infeasible offspring requiring expensive repair. Constraint-aware crossover in MA avoids most of this overhead at the cost of a slightly more complex operator.

4. Computational Cost Analysis

  • Plain GA: O(n) fitness evaluation per individual. Simple and fast per generation but needs many more generations to reach comparable quality.
  • Memetic: O(n²) per individual (n courses × n possible reassignments in 1-opt LS). Each generation is more expensive but each individual is much better. In practice, fewer generations are needed.
  • Verdict: For scheduling, total wall-clock time to reach a target quality level is usually lower for Memetic despite higher per-generation cost.

5. Recommendation

Implement the Memetic Algorithm (Approach C). The scheduling problem's high constraint density and rugged landscape make the GA+LS hybrid the clear winner. The extra per-individual cost of 1-opt local search is more than offset by the higher quality of each generation's population, faster convergence to good schedules, and dramatically fewer infeasible solutions that need repair.
  1. State the search space size to justify needing a metaheuristic over exact methods.
  2. Explain that constraint-aware crossover reduces infeasible offspring — key for highly constrained problems.
  3. Justify the local search addition: scheduling landscapes are rugged with many similar local optima near the global optimum.
  4. Mention the bi-objective nature — weighted sum acceptable, NSGA-II if the full conflict/utilization tradeoff curve is needed.
  5. Conclude: Memetic Algorithm is the recommended approach, with the tradeoff of higher per-generation cost justified by faster convergence.

Problem 2 — Warehouse Order Picking Optimization (30 pts)

A large e-commerce warehouse has m aisles each containing k shelves. A picker starts at a depot, collects a set of p products from various shelf locations, and returns to the depot. Multiple orders may share products stored in the same aisle. The warehouse manager wants to minimize total walking distance for the picker while maximizing the number of orders completed per hour. Orders arrive in batches every 5 minutes. Each batch may contain 3–12 items from different aisles.

Method 1: S-shape routing — traverse each required aisle completely end-to-end in a serpentine pattern
Method 2: Return routing — enter each aisle only as far as the last required item, then return to the main aisle
Method 3 (proposed): Metaheuristic-optimized routing — find the globally optimal traversal order and depth per aisle
Part (a) — 10 pts Model the warehouse order picking problem with a suitable metaheuristic — define representation and fitness ▼

Chosen Algorithm: Tabu Search (TS)

Warehouse picking is structurally a variant of the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) — visit a set of locations (shelf positions) in minimal total distance, starting and ending at the depot. Tabu Search is an excellent choice here because: (1) the neighborhood structure (swap two stops in the route) is natural and efficient to compute, (2) TS's memory mechanisms prevent cycling between similar routes, and (3) TS consistently produces near-optimal TSP solutions with less parameter tuning than GA.

Solution Representation

A solution is a permutation of the p product locations the picker must visit, starting and ending at the depot:

// Route representation (permutation of location IDs): Solution: [depot → loc₃ → loc₇ → loc₁ → loc₅ → loc₂ → depot] Encoded as permutation: [ 3, 7, 1, 5, 2 ] // Each location ID maps to (aisle_id, shelf_depth): loc₃ = (aisle 2, depth 4.5m) loc₇ = (aisle 5, depth 1.2m) ...

Fitness Function

// Minimize total walking distance: f(x) = dist(depot → x₁) + Σ dist(xᵢ → xᵢ₊₁) + dist(xₚ → depot) // Distance between two shelf locations (Manhattan distance in warehouse): dist(locᵢ, locⱼ) = |aisle_i - aisle_j| × aisle_width + |depth_i - depth_j| // Secondary objective (throughput proxy): Orders per hour ≈ 3600 / f(x) [maximize by minimizing f(x)]

Note: both objectives reduce to a single metric here — minimizing walking distance directly maximizes orders per hour. This is a single-objective problem in disguise.

Tabu Search Specifics

ComponentDesign
Neighborhood2-opt moves: reverse the sub-sequence between positions i and j in the route. Produces O(p²) neighbors per iteration.
Tabu listStore the (i,j) pairs of recently reversed sub-sequences. Tabu tenure = 7–10 iterations (prevents revisiting recent routes).
Aspiration criterionAllow a tabu move if it produces a route shorter than the best found so far.
Stopping criterion5 minutes per batch (hard real-time constraint of the warehouse system).
Part (b) — 10 pts What does Method 3 (metaheuristic-optimized routing) propose that Methods 1 and 2 cannot achieve? ▼

What Methods 1 and 2 Are

S-shape (Method 1) and Return (Method 2) are both heuristic routing policies — fixed rules that determine how to traverse aisles without considering the specific layout of items in the current order batch.

  • S-shape: Always traverse required aisles completely, alternating direction. Simple and predictable but wastes distance when items are only at one end of an aisle.
  • Return: Enter each aisle only as far as needed. Better than S-shape for sparse orders but still visits aisles in a fixed order (left-to-right) without optimizing the inter-aisle traversal order.

What Method 3 Adds

Method 3 optimizes both: (1) the order in which aisles are visited, and (2) the entry/exit strategy per aisle (traverse fully, enter from top, enter from bottom) — simultaneously, and specifically for the current batch's item locations.

This is only possible with a metaheuristic because the optimal aisle visit order depends on the specific item positions in the current order — it changes with every batch. Fixed routing policies cannot adapt to this per-batch variability.

Part (c) — 10 pts Pre-implementation analysis: compare all three methods across relevant dimensions and recommend one ▼

1. Solution Quality

Empirical studies on warehouse routing show S-shape produces routes 20–40% longer than optimal; Return produces routes 10–25% longer than optimal; metaheuristic methods consistently reach within 1–5% of optimal for typical order sizes.

2. Computation Time vs. Real-Time Constraint

The warehouse has a 5-minute batch window. Tabu Search with 2-opt on p≤12 items runs in well under 1 second on modern hardware — leaving 4+ minutes to spare. The real-time constraint is not a limiting factor for Method 3.

3. Implementation Complexity

4. Scalability

As order batch size grows (p=12 → p=50 items), S-shape and Return performance degrades predictably but remains suboptimal. TS scales well — 2-opt on p=50 still runs in milliseconds, and solution quality remains near-optimal.

5. Recommendation

Implement Method 3 (Tabu Search). Given that: the real-time constraint is easily met, implementation complexity is manageable, and the quality improvement (20–40% shorter routes) directly translates to 20–40% higher throughput — the business case is overwhelming. For a high-volume warehouse processing thousands of batches daily, a 20% reduction in picker walking distance compounds to significant cost savings and throughput gains.

Problem 3 — Wireless Sensor Network Placement (30 pts)

An environmental monitoring agency must place s sensors across a geographic region divided into g grid cells. Each sensor covers a circular area of radius r and has a battery lifetime inversely proportional to its transmission power. The agency wants to maximize total coverage of the monitored region while minimizing total energy consumption of the sensor network. A central base station must be reachable from every sensor either directly or through at most 2 relay hops. Budget allows at most s=20 sensors.

Approach A: Greedy placement — place each sensor one at a time at the location that adds the most new coverage
Approach B: PSO — treat sensor coordinates as a continuous vector of 2s dimensions, optimize with swarm
Approach C (proposed): Multi-objective PSO (MOPSO) — explicitly optimize coverage and energy as separate Pareto objectives
Part (a) — 10 pts Model the sensor placement problem with PSO — define particle representation, velocity update, and fitness ▼

Chosen Algorithm: PSO

Sensor placement is a continuous-space optimization problem — coordinates (x,y) are real-valued. PSO is ideal for continuous spaces because its velocity update naturally handles real-valued movements, and its social component (attraction to gBest) efficiently guides all particles toward promising high-coverage regions without requiring a discrete neighborhood structure.

Particle Representation

// Each particle = one candidate placement of all s sensors: Particle position: x = [x₁, y₁, x₂, y₂, ..., xₛ, yₛ] Dimension: 2s (2 coordinates per sensor, s sensors) // Example: s=3 sensors in a 100×100 grid: x = [ 23.4, 67.1, 45.0, 12.8, 78.3, 55.6 ] sensor1 sensor2 sensor3

Velocity Update

// Standard PSO velocity update (applied to all 2s dimensions): v(t+1) = w·v(t) + c₁·r₁·(pBest - x(t)) + c₂·r₂·(gBest - x(t)) x(t+1) = x(t) + v(t+1) // Typical parameter values for sensor placement: w = 0.729 (inertia weight — controls momentum) c₁ = c₂ = 1.496 (cognitive and social acceleration) Clamp velocity: |v| ≤ v_max = (x_max - x_min) / 10

Fitness Function

// Combined objective (single-objective PSO, Approach B): Fitness(x) = w₁ × Coverage(x) - w₂ × Energy(x) - w₃ × Connectivity_penalty(x) Coverage(x) = |union of all sensor coverage areas| / |total region area| Energy(x) = Σ power_i² × active_time_i for all sensors i Connectivity_penalty = λ × number_of_sensors_not_reachable_within_2_hops

Constraint: Connectivity

Every sensor must reach the base station within 2 hops. This is enforced via a penalty term in the fitness function — sensors placed out of relay range receive a large negative fitness contribution, pushing the swarm toward connected configurations.

Part (b) — 10 pts What does MOPSO (Approach C) propose over single-objective PSO (Approach B)? ▼

The Core Difference: One Answer vs. a Full Tradeoff Map

Single-objective PSO (Approach B) collapses coverage and energy into one weighted fitness value. This forces you to pre-commit to weights w₁ and w₂ before running the algorithm — you get exactly one solution, and if the weights were wrong, you have to re-run.

MOPSO proposes: Treat coverage and energy as two separate objectives simultaneously. The algorithm produces an entire Pareto front of non-dominated solutions — each representing a different tradeoff between maximum coverage and minimum energy. The decision-maker can then choose from this front based on the actual deployment context (budget, environment criticality) after seeing the full tradeoff curve.

MOPSO Mechanism

  • p_best: Each particle maintains its personal best position, updated via Pareto dominance — a new position replaces p_best only if it dominates the current p_best in both coverage and energy.
  • g_best (archive): No single gBest. All non-dominated solutions found by any particle are stored in an external archive. Each particle selects its social attractor from the archive using crowding distance (to maintain diversity along the Pareto front).
  • Output: A set of 20–100 non-dominated sensor placement configurations, spanning from "maximum coverage, high energy" to "minimum energy, partial coverage."

Why This Is Valuable for Sensor Deployment

In real deployments, the "right" tradeoff changes: a sensor network monitoring a forest fire requires maximum coverage; one monitoring routine soil conditions may prioritize battery life for years of autonomous operation. MOPSO gives the agency all options at once — single-objective PSO forces a premature commitment.

Problem 4 — Job Shop Scheduling Problem (30 pts)

A factory has m machines and must process n jobs. Each job consists of a sequence of operations that must be performed in a fixed order on specific machines (e.g., job 1: machine 2 → machine 5 → machine 1). Each operation has a known processing time. A machine can only process one operation at a time. The factory wants to minimize the makespan (total time to complete all jobs) while minimizing maximum machine idle time (to keep utilization high). This is the classic Job Shop Scheduling Problem (JSSP) — one of the hardest NP-complete problems known.

Approach A: List scheduling — assign operations to machines as they become available using a priority rule (e.g., shortest processing time first)
Approach B: GA with operation-sequence chromosome encoding
Approach C (proposed): GA with disjunctive graph representation and critical path–guided mutation
Part (a) — 10 pts Model the JSSP with a GA — explain the chromosome representation and fitness function carefully ▼

Why JSSP Needs Careful Representation Design

JSSP is notoriously difficult to represent because a chromosome must encode the ordering of operations on each machine while simultaneously respecting the fixed operation order within each job. A naive binary or real-valued encoding cannot capture both constraints cleanly.

Chromosome Representation: Operation-Based Encoding (Approach B)

The most widely used representation for JSSP GA is the operation-based permutation:

// For n=3 jobs, m=3 machines (3 operations per job = 9 total operations): Each job ID appears exactly m times in the chromosome. The k-th occurrence of job id j represents the k-th operation of job j. Chromosome: [ 2, 1, 3, 2, 1, 3, 1, 2, 3 ] Reading left to right: - 1st occurrence of job 2 → operation 1 of job 2 (on its first required machine) - 1st occurrence of job 1 → operation 1 of job 1 - 1st occurrence of job 3 → operation 1 of job 3 - 2nd occurrence of job 2 → operation 2 of job 2 ... and so on. // Key property: any permutation of this chromosome is AUTOMATICALLY VALID // because the k-th appearance of job j always maps to operation k of job j. // No repair needed after crossover or mutation!

Decoding: Active Schedule Generation

To decode a chromosome into an actual schedule: read operations left to right. For each operation, schedule it on its required machine at the earliest possible time (considering both machine availability and the completion of the previous operation of that job). This produces an active schedule — no machine ever idles unnecessarily.

Fitness Function

// Primary objective — minimize makespan (total completion time): f₁(x) = Cmax = max(completion_time_j) for all jobs j // Secondary objective — minimize maximum machine idle time: f₂(x) = max(idle_time_machine_k) for all machines k // Combined fitness (minimize both): Fitness(x) = w₁ × Cmax + w₂ × max_idle_time [minimize]

Part (b) — 10 pts What does the critical path–guided mutation in Approach C propose, and why is it smarter than random mutation? ▼

Background: The Critical Path in JSSP

In any JSSP schedule, the critical path is the longest chain of operations from the start to the makespan. It determines Cmax — the total schedule length. Only operations on the critical path can reduce the makespan if rescheduled. Operations not on the critical path are irrelevant to makespan improvement.

Key insight: Standard random mutation wastes most of its mutations on operations that are NOT on the critical path — these swaps can never reduce Cmax. They just shuffle irrelevant operations and consume generations without progress.

Critical Path–Guided Mutation (Approach C)

Instead of randomly swapping any two genes, the guided mutation:

  1. Decode the current chromosome to extract the actual schedule and compute the critical path.
  2. Identify the critical operations — those on the critical path.
  3. Select a mutation gene pair where at least one gene is a critical operation.
  4. Perform the swap — directly attacking the bottleneck operations that determine Cmax.

The Disjunctive Graph

Approach C also uses a disjunctive graph as the underlying data structure. In this graph, each operation is a node; conjunctive edges (fixed) encode job operation order; disjunctive edges (variable) encode machine access order. A chromosome corresponds to an orientation of all disjunctive edges. The critical path is the longest path in this directed graph — efficiently computed by topological sort in O(operations + edges) time.

1. Why JSSP Is Hard

JSSP with n jobs and m machines has a search space of (n!)^m possible schedules. For n=10, m=10: (10!)^10 ≈ 10^65. Exact methods (branch-and-bound) work for small instances (n,m ≤ 15) but are intractable beyond that. Metaheuristics are essential for practical sizes.

2. Solution Quality Comparison

MethodTypical gap from optimalWhy
List scheduling (A)10–30%Greedy single-pass — no look-ahead, no recovery from poor early decisions
Plain GA (B)2–8%Global search explores broadly but random mutation wastes many generations
Guided-mutation GA (C)0.5–3%Every mutation is targeted at the actual bottleneck — much more efficient use of the search budget

3. Computational Cost

Guided mutation adds O(n×m) per individual per generation (for critical path computation). For typical JSSP sizes (n=m=20), this is ≈400 operations per mutation — negligible on modern hardware. The reduced number of generations needed to reach comparable quality easily offsets this overhead.

4. The Key Tradeoff: Exploitation vs. Exploration

Targeting critical path operations is a strong exploitation bias. This speeds up convergence but can cause premature convergence if the algorithm gets stuck near a local optimum whose critical path structure is misleading. Mitigation: use random mutation for 20% of mutation events to maintain some diversification.

5. Recommendation

Implement Approach C (guided-mutation GA) with the following design:
  1. Operation-based permutation chromosome — guaranteed validity without repair.
  2. PPX crossover — specifically designed for JSSP, preserves precedences from both parents.
  3. 80% critical path–guided mutation + 20% random mutation — exploits bottlenecks while maintaining diversity.
  4. Tournament selection (k=2) — conservative pressure on a rugged JSSP landscape.
  5. Active schedule decoder — ensures no unnecessary idle time in any decoded schedule.
